Deliverable 02 · Propaganda strategy

Shift the belief before the pitch.

Most founders of growing companies don't think they have a promotion problem. They trust their managers. This strategy explains how Sphyr changes that belief, and why we use the tactics we do.

Team 03 · Aryan, Abhinav, Devansh, Bheema · Product Marketing PD2.501, IIIT Hyderabad

1. Summary

We use "propaganda" in the course sense: organised communication that shapes what an audience believes, so they act on it. Our audience is founders of growing companies (50–300 people) and the Heads of People who work with them. Today they believe "my managers know who deserves a promotion." We want them to believe "one person's view is a guess; everyone's view over time is evidence."

We are not trying to sell software first. Our earlier coursework showed that when a CEO trusts his manager's judgement and sees no problem, a product pitch fails. The belief has to move first. Once it has, Sphyr is the obvious next step: a two-minute walkthrough, then a ₹15,000 paid pilot.

Promote on merit, not politics.
Built from everyone who actually worked with them, not one manager's memory. That's Sphyr.

2. The audience we're influencing

RoleWhoWhat they need from us
Decision makerFounders and CEOs of growing companies with 50–300 people. They still sign off every promotion but no longer work next to most of the people they promote. Mid-size IT services and digital engineering firms in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Delhi NCR and Chennai are the core.A reason to doubt the current system that doesn't insult them or their managers.

Who we are not targeting, and why

  • Companies under ~50 people. The founder still sees most of the work. The belief "I know who's good" is still mostly true, so there's nothing to shift.
  • Large enterprises with mature HR suites. They already run formal 360s through established vendors. Their buying cycle doesn't fit a ₹15,000 pilot.
  • Companies that want monitoring. If a buyer wants activity tracking, Sphyr is the wrong tool. Chasing them would damage the "feedback, not surveillance" trust we need with employees.
  • Buyers who want to replace their HRMS. Sphyr runs alongside the HR system. Promising otherwise would break our pricing and packaging.

3. The belief we have to shift

TodayAfter the campaign
Belief"My managers know their people. Their judgement is enough.""A manager is one view. People should be judged by everyone who sees their work."
AttitudeComfortable. Feedback tools seem like admin, or like surveillance.Uneasy about who they might be missing. Curious about pooled, anonymous feedback.
ResponseNo search, no action.Try the two-minute walkthrough, then start a pilot.

Why the current belief holds. It used to be true. At 40 people the founder saw everyone's work. Growth removed that visibility gradually, so there was never a moment that felt like a problem. Founders also trust the managers they hired, so any message that sounds like "your managers are biased" gets rejected.

How we shift it. We blame the structure, not the people: one view, once a year, can't capture work spread across many colleagues. That lets founders change their mind without admitting they or their managers were wrong. The team settled on this early. We never say founders "approve promotions blind". We say the company grew faster than anyone's line of sight.

4. Central message and why it works

LevelMessageUsed where
PromisePromote on merit, not politics.Every artefact: site hero, ad headline, all three LinkedIn posts
Core lineBuilt from everyone who actually worked with them, not one manager's memory. That's Sphyr.Home closing, walkthrough ending, Post 2
Proof pointsTwo minutes a fortnight · anonymous · everyone in the Sphere · ₹149 flatAd, how it works, pricing, Post 3
Reasons to believeGalton's crowd (1906); rater-effect research (Scullen et al., 2000); 2018 pilot of the methodHome evidence, Post 2

Why "merit, not politics". Founders say they want a meritocracy. "Politics" names the thing they dislike without accusing anyone. The contrast is short, easy to remember and easy to repeat. Why "two-minute", "anonymous" and "everyone". These three words answer the three objections in advance: it's too much work, people won't be honest, and it's one more opinion. A competitor would struggle to claim all three, which is why we keep them in every piece of copy.

Fallback: if testing shows "politics" puts managers on the defensive, we switch to "merit, not memory". It carries the same idea with less edge. The ad includes this as a planned A/B variant.

5. Persuasion tactics, and why they fit founders

TacticHow we use itWhy it fits this audience
ReframingPresent the problem as "growth outpaced sight", a structural issue, not a personal failing.Founders defend their managers. A reframe lets them change their view without losing face.
Identification through storyThe quiet contributor (Priya) who unblocks three teams and still leaves. Clearly labelled illustrative.Every founder has lost someone like her. A specific person is more persuasive than a statistic and is more likely to be shared.
Scientific authorityGalton's 787 guesses, and the finding that more than half of rating variance comes from the rater (Scullen, Mount & Goff, 2000).Our founders run engineering firms and respond to evidence more than adjectives. Authority from published research, not from us.
Visual contrastOne dot (one manager) against a ring of fourteen (the Sphere). Used on the site, in Post 2 and in the ad.It explains the idea in about two seconds in a fast-scrolling feed.
Self-persuasionThe walkthrough asks the visitor to pick a candidate before seeing the pooled data.When people find a gap in their own judgement, they believe it more than anything we could tell them.
RepetitionThe same promise and core line in every artefact, in the same words.A busy founder sees each piece once. Repetition across pieces is what makes the message stick.
Transparency as risk reductionPublic price, refund terms, anonymity rules and data handling, with no form to fill and no sales call.Meera's journey map put the main friction at trial signup and the gap before first value. Openness removes reasons to delay.
Earned social proof (not faked)The first 25 customers get a two-year price lock in exchange for a case study.We don't have customer logos yet and won't pretend to. The case-study trade builds real proof for later phases.

8. How the persona shaped each choice

Meera is Head of People at a growing IT services company of about 250 people. She reports to a founder who signs off every promotion. Her 12-step journey map showed three friction points: trial signup, onboarding, and the gap before first value. These are the choices she drove:

What we know about MeeraWhat we did
She feels the problem first, in exit interviews, but the founder decides.Post 1 is written so she can forward it to him. The site speaks to founders but gives Meera the evidence.
She must protect employees' trust.A dedicated trust page: who sees what, the three anonymity rules, no surveillance.
She avoids sales calls and gated content.CTA everywhere is "try the 2-minute walkthrough": no sign-up, no form to read anything.
She has to justify spend to a founder.Public flat price with the reasoning shown, a pilot credited against the first bill, and a refund promise.
She uses LinkedIn for professional learning.LinkedIn is the main channel for both organic posts and the ad.
Plain-speaking, wary of HR jargon.Short sentences, no "talent intelligence" buzzwords, honest labels on every story and statistic.

Retention is built into the product. Every fortnight, each employee gets new cards, so Sphyr is part of the routine instead of a once-a-year login. The refund promise tells the customer when to judge us: after six cycles. That gives us a clear first-value target to work towards.

10. How this connects to positioning

For founders of 50–300-person companies who sign off on promotions but can't see who did what, Sphyr is a 360-degree feedback layer alongside their HR system that lets them promote on merit, not politics, because it pools two-minute anonymized ratings every fortnight from everyone an employee works with.

Each part of the positioning has a job in this strategy. "Can't see who did what" is the belief we unsettle. "Merit, not politics" is the promise we repeat. "Two-minute, anonymized, everyone" are the reasons to believe we prove. "Alongside their HR system" removes the fear of a big switch, which is why the pilot is small and paid, not a migration.

11. Expected response

We want the founder to move from "we don't have this problem" to "I want to see what my people see," and to show it by doing three things:

  1. Engage: comment, share or forward a post. This is especially likely from Heads of People sending it to founders.
  2. Try: complete the two-minute walkthrough.
  3. Commit: start a ₹15,000 six-week pilot on one team.

Business context: break-even is about 67 customers of 100 employees at ₹149. The first-25 case-study offer is meant to get us a third of the way there, with proof we can use for the rest.

12. Objections we expect

ObjectionOur answer
"My managers are good. This sounds like you don't trust them."Managers still decide. Sphyr gives them more than one view. Research shows over half of any rating reflects the rater. That's true of good raters too.
"People will gang up or rate their friends."In the 2018 pilot of the method, the tendency to collude dropped to 0% after three weeks. Pooling across a whole Sphere dilutes a few friendly ratings.
"Employees will think it's surveillance."It only knows what colleagues choose to say. No activity tracking. Nobody sees who wrote what.
"Another tool everyone has to learn."Two minutes a fortnight, about five clicks per card. It runs alongside the current HR system.
"What if it doesn't work for us?"A ₹15,000 pilot first. On the annual plan, a full refund if the first six cycles show nothing new.

13. Guardrails

Propaganda can mislead. Ours must stay honest, because Sphyr sells fairness and evidence. If we're caught exaggerating, the main message falls apart.

  • No invented customers, testimonials or statistics. Illustrative stories are labelled as such.
  • Pilot numbers are always labelled as 2018 pen-and-paper results for the method, not for today's software.
  • We never name or blame managers. We criticise the structure, not the people.
  • Competitor comparisons only use prices we checked at the source, with the date.
  • No fear selling about attrition numbers we can't support.
