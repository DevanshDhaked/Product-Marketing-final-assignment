## 1. The professor's version against the standard model

| | Professor's course notes | Standard AIDA (Lewis) | Main variants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 | Awareness: the customer wants "info" about everything around the product | Attention: "attract attention" | Many modern versions also say Awareness (Wikipedia: "Some of the contemporary variants of the model replace attention with awareness") |
| Stage 2 | Interest: the customer wants "assurance" (guarantee, refund or cashback, a manual) | Interest: "maintain interest" | AIDCA / modified AIDA add Conviction or Confidence before or after Desire |
| Stage 3 | Decision: do give gift coupons for the next purchase; do not make them fill details manually or add payment friction | Desire: "arouse desire" | "AIDA, Careca do Marketing model: Attention, Interest, Decision, Action" (Wikipedia variants list, citing Moreira 2024) |
| Stage 4 | Action | Action: "get action" | AIDAS adds Satisfaction (Sheldon 1911); AISDALSLove adds Search, Like, Share, Love |
| Extra | CAC: money spent to convert one customer; consider expectations and norms | none | none |

Origin. Wikipedia attributes the model to E. St. Elmo Lewis and quotes his 1910 talk: "All advertising must attract attention, maintain interest, arouse desire, get action." A three-step version appeared anonymously in Printers' Ink on 9 February 1898. Strong's 1925 book made the Lewis attribution common. A 1920 selling manual (Kitson) gives a longer seller sequence: "Secure attention. Hold attention Through Interest. Arouse Desire. Create Confidence and Belief. Secure Decision and Action. Create Satisfaction."

B2B journeys. Gartner (buyer enablement ebook, 2019) says "Virtually every B2B purchase spans six distinct jobs": problem identification, solution exploration, requirements building, supplier selection, validation, consensus creation. These are not a funnel: "90% of all buyers reported revisiting, or looping back, to at least one job". Buyers spend "Only 17% of buying time" in supplier meetings, so the page and the content do most of the selling.

Where the professor differs, and what "Decision" adds over "Desire":
- His stages describe what the customer wants at each step (info, assurance), not what the ad does to the reader. That is a buyer's view, closer to Gartner's jobs than to Lewis's checklist.
- He puts assurance in Interest. Lewis and Kitson put confidence later (after desire). Gartner calls it validation.
- "Decision" replaces an emotion (desire) with a step the buyer takes, and his do and don't list is about removing friction at that step: no manual forms, no payment friction, a reason to come back. Desire asks "do they want it?"; Decision asks "is it easy and safe to say yes?" Kitson's "Secure Decision and Action" and the Careca variant show the word has precedent.
- CAC ties the journey to cost. Standard AIDA has no cost term.

Which reading to follow for grading. Follow his: Attention, Interest, Decision, Action. In the v2 review he wrote "AIDA - Attention, Interest, Decision, Action" and for the ad "missed the first A of AIDA. what will make someone stop and look (attention)". His class-notes "Awareness" (customer wants info about everything around the product) then becomes the job of the first screen: stop the reader and tell them what this is. Use his Interest meaning (assurance), and his Decision meaning (low friction), not Desire.

## 2. Attention for a senior B2B reader

What the sources say:
- Headline does most of the work. Ogilvy: "On average, five times as many people read the headlines as read the body copy." (two independent notes pages agree).
- Name the brand in the headline: "If you don't, 80% of readers (who don't read the body copy) will never know what product you are advertising."
- Flag the reader: "put a word in your headline that will flag them down (e.g. asthma, bedwetters, women over thirty-five)." For Sphyr that word is the reader's own: consulting firm, partner, appraisal, engagement (see the vocabulary research).
- No puns: "Don't write tricky headlines (double meanings, puns). Readers travel too fast through the jungle of a magazine." Another notes page: "Your headline should telegraph what you want to say."
- Ten seconds on the web. NN/g: "Users often leave Web pages in 10 to 20 seconds ... you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds." (I found no primary source for a "5 second test"; use the NN/g 10 seconds.)
- First screen gets most of the looking. NN/g 2018: 57% of viewing time above the fold (80% in 2010), 74% in the first two screenfuls. Within the first screen, more than 65% of time goes to the top half. NN/g F-pattern: eyes go to the top and the left first, so the first words of the headline and of each line carry the load.
- Awareness level sets the opening. Schwartz via Copyhackers: Most Aware ("only needs to know the deal"), Product-Aware, Solution-Aware, Problem-Aware ("senses he has a problem, but doesn't know there's a solution"), Completely Unaware. Copyhackers' rule: "Start with your value proposition in your hero section." Inference: a head of talent at a 40 to 400 person firm is problem-aware (appraisals depend on one manager's memory) and not product-aware (nobody knows Sphyr). So the first screen needs both the problem in her words and a plain statement of what Sphyr is.
- B2B print precedent. McGraw-Hill's 1958 business-press ad "The man in the chair" stops the reader by speaking as the buyer: "I don't know who you are. I don't know your company. ... Now, what was it you wanted to sell me?" Attention came from naming the buyer's own doubt, not from wordplay.

Rules that follow (for the page hero and the ad headline):
1. In about 10 seconds the reader can say what Sphyr is, for whom, and what to do next.
2. The headline or the line directly under it names the reader (consulting firm, partners, head of talent) and the job (appraisals, promotions).
3. Brand name visible in the first screen of the page and in the ad.
4. One specific concrete detail beats an abstract line (a number, a moment in the appraisal cycle, a role).
5. No line that needs a second line to explain it (the professor's "explain a joke" rule matches Ogilvy's no-puns rule).

## 4. Decision: low friction, and what a "gift coupon" means in B2B

- Form length. HubSpot (40,000+ landing pages): 3-field forms converted best (slightly over 25%), then 5 fields (above 21%); drop-downs and multi-line fields cost more than single text fields. CXL counter-evidence: in some tests removing fields lowered conversion, so ask only for what the call needs, not the minimum possible. I could not verify the often-quoted Imagescape "11 to 4 fields" figures and leave them out.
- Payment at the first step. 0 of 10 home pages ask for payment; the first step is always a demo, a conversation or a trial. Inference: for Sphyr the first step is a call, and payment only comes at the pilot order.
- Calendar booking. Several vendors link "Book a demo" straight to a booking flow. I did not find independent data on calendar booking against forms; treat as common practice, not evidence.
- Prefilled demo. A walkthrough the reader can open without a form is the lowest-friction Decision step the team already has (`site/demo.html`).
- "Gift coupon for the next purchase" in B2B maps to, in order of fit:

| Consumer idea | B2B form | Status for Sphyr |
|---|---|---|
| Coupon on the next purchase | Pilot fee credited to the first annual bill | Already decided (Rs 25,000 credited) |
| Extra free goods | Extended pilot or free cycles | Not decided |
| Coupon for a friend | Referral credit between firms | Not decided; segment firms know each other (inference) |
| Loyalty reward | Renewal credit | Was in v1, later not adopted |

## 5. Action: one clear next step

- Primary CTA wording seen: "Book a demo" or "Request a demo" (7 of 10), "Talk to an expert" (Betterworks; Keka shows it beside "Book a demo"), "Schedule a Demo" (Darwinbox). Pricing is the usual second link (9 of 10).
- Pattern seen on 15Five, Small Improvements and Engagedly: the same primary CTA in the hero and again at the end of the page, with pricing as a quieter second option. 15Five closes with "Ready to drive extraordinary performance?" plus "Book a Demo" and "See Pricing".
- Single CTA: Unbounce's figure of 13.5% average conversion for single-CTA pages is widely quoted but I only found it second hand. Tinuiti argues the one-CTA rule fits home and top-of-funnel pages, while high-intent pages can carry more options.
- Rule that follows: one primary action, repeated (hero and end); one secondary link (pricing or walkthrough); no third competing ask in the same view. For Sphyr the primary action is the 20-minute call; the walkthrough is the secondary.

## 6. CAC for a zero-budget campaign with one small paid ad

CAC is sales and marketing spend divided by the customers won in the same period (HubSpot). With no media budget, the money CAC is the one small ad plus the pilot cost the firm does not recover. Founder or team time is the real cost and it should be counted, or CAC looks near zero and misleads. The common benchmark is LTV at least 3 times CAC (HubSpot and others, traced to David Skok); for a small seller to firms of 40 to 400 people that only matters once there are a few paying firms. Expectations and norms matter in three places: the buyer's norm is a demo or call before any payment, so the ad's job is a booked call, not a sale; a priced pilot that is credited back is the segment's normal way to try, and it keeps the pilot from becoming a hidden acquisition cost; and the success measure for one ad should be calls booked per rupee, since one won firm at the pilot stage is too small a sample for a CAC figure. The CAC number itself is an estimate the team must label as its own.

## 7. Running A, I, D, A on one page, one ad and one post

### One page: section order

| Order | Stage | Must answer | Length (guide) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Attention (first screen) | What is this, for whom, why now, what to do | Headline plus one line plus one CTA; fits one screen |
| 2 | Interest, part 1: the problem in their words | "Is this my problem?" One scene from the appraisal cycle | 2 to 4 short lines |
| 3 | Interest, part 2: how it works and assurance | What happens every two weeks; what partners see; what is included (setup, training, materials); data terms | 3 steps, one screenshot each |
| 4 | Decision | Price, pilot, what happens if we do not sign, how little the first step asks | One card each for plan and pilot; 3-field form or a booking link |

### One print ad

AIDA compresses into three parts:
- Attention is the headline. It names the reader or their moment, carries the brand or sits next to it, and has no pun (Ogilvy). The McGraw-Hill ad shows a B2B headline can be the buyer's own voice.
- Interest and Decision are the body: two or three short lines that say what Sphyr is and one assurance (pilot terms or what is included).
- Action is one call with one address (URL or QR to the 20-minute call). Ogilvy: most readers never read the body, so the headline plus brand must work alone.

### One LinkedIn post

- The first lines are the Attention. Desktop shows about 210 characters before "see more", mobile about 140 or 3 lines (tool vendors, not LinkedIn docs). The hook must work in that space.
- LinkedIn ranks on dwell time: its engineers model the chance a member skips an update (LinkedIn Engineering, 2020). A post that holds the reader past the fold is shown more (inference from the ranking model).
- The professor's point: people are there "to make reputation points". The Edelman-LinkedIn 2024 survey (sponsored, about 3,500 respondents) says 75% of buyers and C-suite leaders say a piece of thought leadership led them to research a product they were not considering. Interest on LinkedIn is an idea the reader would be glad to reshare, not a product pitch.
- Decision and Action: one line that makes the next step small, then one link. Keep the price off the post (the team already did this, commit 53081a0).
- Gap: no real LinkedIn post was read, because LinkedIn needs a login. The structure above is from the sources, not from a sampled post.

## Stage checklist

| Stage | What the reader wants | What the page must show | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attention | To know in 10 seconds what this is and whether it is for them | Brand, what Sphyr is, for whom (consulting firm, head of talent, partners), one CTA, in the first screen | Puns, slogans that need a second line, words the app does not use ("file"), AI or any do-not-claim item |
| Interest | Info and assurance: is this my problem, how does it work, what if it fails | The problem in their words; how a cycle works; what partners see; setup, training and materials included; pilot terms and data export | Long explanations of a phrase, monitoring language, invented proof or logos |
| Decision | To say yes without risk or effort | Price and pilot on one card each; credit of the pilot fee; what happens if they do not sign; a 3-field form or booking link | Payment at the first step, long forms, drop-downs, pressure tactics or countdowns |
| Action | One obvious next step | The same primary CTA as the hero (20-minute call), one secondary link | Several competing asks in one view, a CTA label that differs page to page |

## What this means for v3

- Use the professor's four stages, Attention, Interest, Decision, Action, as section names in the team's plan, and his meanings: Interest is assurance, Decision is low friction.
- First screen: brand, a plain line that says Sphyr is peer feedback for appraisals at consulting firms, and one CTA. Test it on one person outside the team with a 10-second look.
- Ad: the headline must work without the body, carry or sit beside the brand, and name the reader; the tagline can sit under it, not replace it (pending the user's call).
- Interest section: show "setup, training and training materials included" as the manual-type assurance, plus the pilot terms and the data export and deletion line. The user still decides refund or guarantee against pilot framing (section 3).
- Decision section: plan and pilot cards next to each other, the credit to the first bill stated as the B2B "coupon", and a form of 3 fields or a booking link.
